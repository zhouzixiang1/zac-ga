\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Zoned Neutral-Atom Architecture}

A spatial light modulator (SLM) creates static optical traps in both the storage and entanglement zones. Dense storage traps hold atoms outside the Rydberg illumination region. Entanglement-zone traps form gate-site pairs, each holding two atoms that receive a global Rydberg pulse to execute a controlled-Z (CZ) gate~\cite{evered2023highfidelity,lin2025zac}. Addressable Raman beams implement single-qubit rotations. Global pulses also excite entanglement-zone atoms outside the target gates, so inter-layer residency trades trap-transfer losses against additional idle-excitation losses~\cite{evered2023highfidelity,lin2025zac}.

An acousto-optic deflector (AOD) controls beam deflection along two orthogonal axes to form a mobile optical-trap array with adjustable row and column positions. Atoms transfer from SLM traps to AOD traps, travel with the mobile traps, and transfer into destination SLM traps~\cite{bluvstein2022coherenttransport,lin2025zac}. In Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture-preliminaries}(a), $q_0$ and $q_1$ execute a two-qubit gate in the entanglement zone, while $q_2$ receives a single-qubit rotation in storage. Atom $q_3$ travels from storage to the entanglement zone using the AOD.

Parallel transport within one AOD is subject to two constraint classes~\cite{stade2024abstractmodel,stade2025routingaware}. The first is the \emph{row/column relation constraint}. Active rows and columns preserve their order during motion, and atoms sharing a row or column must retain that relationship. Let $(x_i,y_i)$ and $(x_j,y_j)$ denote two source positions, with destinations $(x'_i,y'_i)$ and $(x'_j,y'_j)$. Assigning both transports to one batch requires, for every $\star\in\{<,=,>\}$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x_i\star x_j&\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x'_i\star x'_j,\\
y_i\star y_j&\ \Longleftrightarrow\ y'_i\star y'_j.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:aod-relations}
\end{equation}
This condition excludes both order reversal and changes in row or column membership. Transports that merge distinct source rows into one destination row cannot execute concurrently, even without intersecting paths, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture-preliminaries}(b).

The second is the \emph{unintended-intersection constraint}. Simultaneously activated AOD rows and columns form traps at every intersection; those not used for the intended trap transfers are termed ghost spots. These unintended traps must not overlap stationary atoms during trap transfers. In Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture-preliminaries}(c), picking up $q_0$ and $q_1$ together creates a ghost spot at $q_2$. Separate pickups in $B_1$ and $B_2$ avoid this interference. The compiler groups compatible transports into rearrangement batches, which execute sequentially, with the longest trajectory determining each batch's duration.

Batches must also respect SLM trap-occupancy dependencies. In Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture-preliminaries}(c), $B_1$ picks up $q_0$ and marks its source trap as vacated. That site can be occupied as soon as $q_0$ transfers into a mobile trap; the relevant instant is source-trap release, not completion of $q_0$'s remaining trajectory~\cite{lin2025zac}. The same panel keeps $q_2$ in its original SLM trap $s$. Batches $B_1$ and $B_2$ only serialize the pickups of $q_0$ and $q_1$; they do not make $q_2$ leave $s$. If another atom requires $s$ as a destination, $q_2$ must be relocated or a different trap chosen. Reordering these two batches cannot release $s$.

We evaluate compilation results with the fidelity model for zoned neutral-atom architectures~\cite{lin2025zac}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\log F={}&N_1\log f_1+N_2\log f_2
+N_{\mathrm{exc}}\log f_{\mathrm{exc}}\\
&+N_{\mathrm{tran}}\log f_{\mathrm{tran}}
+\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q}\log\left(1-\frac{t_q}{T_2}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
Here, $N_1$ and $N_2$ count single- and two-qubit gates, respectively. $N_{\mathrm{exc}}$ counts excitations of nonparticipating atoms in the entanglement zone, and $N_{\mathrm{tran}}$ counts per-atom trap transfers between SLM and AOD traps. The corresponding per-operation fidelity factors are $f_1$, $f_2$, $f_{\mathrm{exc}}$, and $f_{\mathrm{tran}}$. $t_q$ denotes the accumulated idle time of atom $q$, and $T_2$ denotes the coherence time. With fixed gate counts, compilation primarily improves fidelity by reducing trap transfers, idle-atom excitation, and coherence losses. Following Stade et al.'s routing evaluation~\cite{stade2025routingaware}, we report rearrangement batches and rearrangement time. The batch count measures the number of compatible trajectory groups requiring sequential execution; fewer batches indicate greater routing parallelism. Rearrangement time sums critical-trajectory durations across batches, measuring the total time required for atom transport.

\subsection{Neutral-Atom Compilation Methods}

When atom positions remain largely fixed, compilers partition circuits and choose initial layouts to match native gate types and static connectivity. Patel et al.~\cite{patel2022geyser} proposed Geyser, which partitions circuits around native multiqubit gates. GRAPHINE~\cite{patel2023graphine} uses weighted interaction graphs to select array positions that match circuit interactions. Schmid et al.~\cite{schmid2024hybrid} combine long-range gates with atom transport for devices that offer several connectivity mechanisms.

Reconfigurable arrays without separate storage and entanglement zones primarily improve connectivity through atom movement. OLSQ-DPQA jointly solves gate scheduling, placement, and movement, while Enola separates two-qubit gate scheduling from parallel transport~\cite{tan2022mapping,tan2024olsqdpqa,tan2025enola}. Atomique and PARALLAX increase parallelism through multiple AOD arrays and SWAP-free movement, respectively~\cite{wang2024atomique,ludmir2024parallax}. Q-Pilot fixes data atoms in static traps and uses reusable mobile ancillary atoms for two-qubit interactions. Weaver supports compilation across different reconfigurable arrays through a dedicated intermediate representation~\cite{wang2024qpilot,kirmemis2025weaver}. These methods focus on realizing gate interactions and executing them in parallel; zoned architectures additionally require storage isolation and inter-zone transport.

For zoned architectures, Lin et al.~\cite{lin2025zac} proposed the reuse-aware compiler ZAC, which retains reusable entanglement-zone atoms through adjacent-layer matching and includes the next two-qubit pairing and its distance in gate- and storage-site matching. Stade et al.~\cite{stade2025routingaware} proposed routing-aware placement on an abstract AOD model~\cite{stade2024abstractmodel}, using A* to search gate and intermediate placements. Its cost includes compatible movement groups, each group's longest trajectory, and the spacing of the atoms that share the next two-qubit gate. The former reduces repeated trap transfers; the latter links site selection to routing parallelism. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2026zap} proposed ZAP, which also targets zoned architectures: layer-weighted initialization with early-movement conflict estimates, then dynamic placement that first decides residency or return from idle-excitation losses before the next use and then assigns sites gate by gate, scoring sequential greedy choices by distance and conflict counts. ZAP's published array spacings, coherence-loss model, and fidelity terms differ from our settings, so its reported values are not compared directly. The experiments compare GA-LK with reuse-aware compilation and routing-aware placement under shared inputs, architecture, and scoring model.

\subsection{Inter-Layer Residency and Storage-Site Selection}

Inter-layer residency determines the zone an atom occupies between two interactions. Remaining in the entanglement zone reduces round-trip transfers, whereas returning to storage avoids Rydberg exposure in intermediate layers. The assigned storage site determines where later transport starts and whether the atom obstructs other paths. Residency, assigned storage sites, and subsequent gate sites jointly determine the physical cost of inter-layer rearrangement.

Whether residency is beneficial depends on when the atom next participates in an interaction. Atom $q$ executes a CZ gate with $p$ in $L_\ell$, then interacts with $r$ in $L_{\ell+3}$. Neither intermediate layer contains a gate involving $q$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline-motivation}(a). Remaining in the entanglement zone exposes $q$ to idle excitation in those two layers. Returning to storage requires re-entry for its next interaction, adding trap transfers and transport, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline-motivation}(b). Residency decisions therefore account for both the layer interval before reuse and the actual round-trip cost.

Return locations also affect the feasible paths of other atoms. In Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline-motivation}(c), $s_1$ and $s_2$ are two distinct static optical traps in the storage array. Returning $q$ to $s_1$ blocks atom $u$'s direct repositioning path in the next layer. Choosing $s_2$ outside the path preserves this transport option. The same atom $u$ is tracked throughout; the hollow circle denotes its destination trap. Both choices require the same current trap-transfer count, but the former requires a different subsequent path or an intermediate stop. Updating atom positions and trap occupancy in future-layer order incorporates this difference into return-site selection.

\subsection{Our Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Reuse-aware compilation decides reuse by adjacent-layer matching, and routing-aware placement selects sites by transport parallelism; both estimate the current cost from the next two-qubit pairing~\cite{lin2025zac,stade2025routingaware}. For residency and return between nonadjacent gate layers, these methods do not jointly search over gate sites, residency, and return storage sites in one candidate, nor compute subsequent trap-transfer, idle-excitation, and coherence losses from the positions, occupancy, and clocks after a candidate is executed. We present GA-LK, a joint placement method with genetic search and multilayer look-ahead.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate the coupled choices of gate sites, residency, and return storage sites as a fidelity-loss minimization problem, and solve it by genetic search and matching. Genetic search jointly selects two-qubit gate sites and each atom's residency or return; matching then assigns mutually distinct storage traps to returning atoms. Each complete assignment is scored by trap-transfer, idle-excitation, and coherence losses.
  \item We incorporate the multilayer execution cost induced by a candidate into the current placement, and compute that cost by decayed multilayer look-ahead. Look-ahead starts from the positions, occupancy, and clocks after the candidate completes the current layer, and evaluates how residency and storage sites affect later interactions. The same execution-cost evaluation is also used at initialization to select an early-layer layout.
  \item We incorporate executable rearrangement construction into placement-candidate evaluation. Before commitment, candidates undergo occupancy, AOD row/column relation, and unintended-intersection checks. Conflict grouping and source-trap release checks form execution sequences, allowing the search to select candidates according to their resulting batches and losses.
\end{itemize}
